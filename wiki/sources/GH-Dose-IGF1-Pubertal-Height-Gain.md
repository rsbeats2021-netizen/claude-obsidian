# GH Dose-Dependent IGF-I Response and Pubertal Height Gain (Lundberg et al. 2015)

A long-running (1988-2009) Swedish multicenter randomized trial in 104 children with idiopathic isolated GH deficiency, followed from GH treatment start to adult height. At puberty onset, children were randomized to continue standard-dose GH (33 μg/kg/day) or receive a higher, more "physiological" dose (67 μg/kg/day, mimicking the natural pubertal rise in GH secretion driven by sex steroids).

The higher pubertal GH dose produced a significantly greater rise in IGF-I during puberty (mean pubertal IGF-I SDS 1.1 vs. 0.9, p=0.031) and, correspondingly, significantly greater pubertal height gain. Prepubertal IGF-I response to GH also mattered: the first-year change in IGF-I after starting GH correlated positively with both prepubertal and total adult height gain, while paradoxically, higher IGF-I levels already present *at the start* of GH treatment were associated with *less* subsequent prepubertal growth (likely reflecting that children with milder GH deficiency, and thus higher baseline IGF-I, have less room for a treatment-induced growth response). In multivariate models, the combination of prepubertal ΔIGF-I and dose-dependent pubertal ΔIGF-I explained roughly a quarter to a third of the variance in height-gain outcomes.

> [!gap] This trial spans two decades (1988-2009) with evolving assay methods; 44% of children in the high-dose group had their dose reduced before completion (patient/physician choice once satisfied with growth), which the authors account for via intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses but which introduces some real-world noise into the dose-response signal.

## Relevance to This Domain

A rigorous human clinical demonstration that the magnitude of the pubertal IGF-I surge — not just its presence — is dose-dependent and directly predicts height outcome, adding a quantitative human clinical anchor to the [[Growth Hormone-IGF-1 Axis]] vocabulary page, which previously only had review-level and single-family case-study evidence for GH-androgen/height interactions.
