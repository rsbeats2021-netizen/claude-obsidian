# Source: Honey for Nutrition and Health: A Review (Bogdanov et al. 2008)

**Type**: Comprehensive review (Journal of the American College of Nutrition, 2008) — the foundational composition reference already cited by several other sources in this vault (e.g. [[Honey-Aerobic-Dance-Bone-Turnover|Ooi et al. 2011]]'s nutrition table)

## Summary

The most complete single composition/physiology review in the [[Health and Nutrition]] domain — covers honey's full nutrient profile, glycemic behavior, and the evidence base (as of 2008) for antimicrobial, antioxidant, antitumor, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, infant-nutrition, and athletic-performance effects.

## Key Findings

- **Composition**: ~95% of dry weight is carbohydrate (mainly fructose 30-45%, glucose 24-40%), plus ~25 minor oligosaccharides, ~0.5% protein/enzymes (diastase, invertase, glucose oxidase), trace vitamins/minerals, and 56-500 mg/kg total polyphenols (mainly flavonoids: quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, apigenin, chrysin, galangin).
- **Glycemic index**: ranges 32 (acacia) to 85 depending on fructose content and botanical source — directly relevant to [[Honey Hypoglycemic Mechanism]]; average across studied honeys ≈55, versus sucrose ≈68 and pure glucose 100.
- **Antimicrobial**: broad-spectrum activity against many gram-positive and some gram-negative bacteria, via low water activity, hydrogen peroxide (glucose oxidase-derived), low pH, and non-peroxide phenolic/flavonoid compounds; peroxide activity degrades with heat/light/storage, non-peroxide activity is more stable.
- **Antioxidant**: ORAC values correlate with total phenolic content; darker honeys (buckwheat) had markedly higher ORAC (~17 µmol TE/g) than light honeys (acacia ~3 µmol TE/g).
- **Cardiovascular**: 75g honey vs. equivalent dextrose/glucose caused lower rises in plasma glucose, insulin, and CRP; reduced LDL-C and triglycerides vs. artificial honey (fructose+glucose mix) in the same study, suggesting non-sugar components matter for lipid effects too.
- **Athletic performance**: honey gels were as effective as (slightly better than) glucose gels for a 64km cycling performance trial; well tolerated, no hypoglycemia observed in fasted or resistance-trained athletes.
- **Infant safety**: honey should not be given to infants under 12 months due to Clostridium botulinum spore risk in an immature gut (rare but documented infant botulism cases); safe after 12 months.
- **Dosing caveat**: nearly all documented positive health effects were observed at single doses of 50-80g — much higher than typical culinary use (a teaspoon is ~7g).
