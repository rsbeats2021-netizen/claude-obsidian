# Honey Composition and Bioactivity

What's actually in honey, and how raw/unprocessed honey differs from heat-processed commercial honey in composition and biological activity.

---

## Baseline Composition

Per [[Honey-Nutrition-Health-Review-2008|Bogdanov et al. 2008]]: ~95% of dry weight is carbohydrate (fructose 30-45%, glucose 24-40%, plus ~25 minor oligosaccharides), ~0.5% protein/enzymes (diastase, invertase, glucose oxidase), and 56-500 mg/kg total polyphenols (mainly flavonoids — quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, apigenin, chrysin, galangin). Glycemic index ranges widely, 32-85, depending on botanical source and fructose content.

## Raw vs. Processed Honey

[[Raw-vs-Regular-Honey-Nigeria|Edo et al. 2022]] found a counterintuitive split when comparing raw and regular (heat-processed) honey:

- **Processing increases measured phenolic/flavonoid/tannin content** — likely via Maillard-reaction melanoidin formation during heating, which can itself be antioxidant-active.
- **But raw honey wins on actual bioactivity** — stronger free-radical scavenging and broader/stronger antibacterial activity than processed honey, in some cases exceeding standard antibiotics against clinical bacterial isolates.
- **Reconciling this**: heat processing is known to deactivate honey's hydrogen-peroxide-based antibacterial mechanism even as it may generate new (different) antioxidant compounds. More phenolic content on paper doesn't guarantee more real-world antibacterial/antioxidant potency — the *type* of compound and its bioavailability matter, not just total mass.
- Both raw and processed honey in this study met all Codex Alimentarius international quality standards — commercial processing did not indicate adulteration, just a different bioactivity profile.

## Why This Matters for Other Honey Claims

Nearly every other paper in [[Health and Nutrition]] — [[Honey Hypoglycemic Mechanism]], [[Honey and Bone Metabolism]] — attributes honey's effects to "its phenolic/flavonoid content" as if this were a fixed, uniform property. This concept page is the reminder that composition varies substantially by:
- Botanical/floral source (chestnut and honeydew honey inhibited PTP1B far more than acacia in [[Honey-PTP1B-Insulin-Signaling|Lori et al. 2019]])
- Processing (raw vs. heated, per above)
- Storage conditions (heat/light/storage degrade peroxide activity faster than non-peroxide activity, per Bogdanov 2008)

## Evidence Strength

> [!gap] Composition studies are largely regional/sample-specific (Nigerian honey in Edo et al., Swiss/European data underlying Bogdanov's 2008 review) — floral source and geography both drive substantial variance, so figures from one study don't necessarily generalize to "honey" as a single substance.
