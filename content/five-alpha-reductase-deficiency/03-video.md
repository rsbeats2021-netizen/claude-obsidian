### [0:00–0:08] HOOK
SPOKEN: Some people can't make DHT at all. A genetic enzyme deficiency blocks

### [0:08–0:18] PROBLEM
SPOKEN: DHT gets credited with a lot in this space — the more potent androgen,
        the one that potentiates growth plate effects. Most of that comes from
        preclinical work.
  [ON-SCREEN: "SO WHAT HAPPENS WITH NONE?"]

### [0:18–0:38] EVIDENCE
SPOKEN: In 5-alpha-reductase type 2 deficiency, testosterone is normal and DHT
        is essentially absent for life. These children were treated with
        androgens — but the goal was genital development, not height. Height
        was recorded alongside, not targeted. Which means the data can't
        cleanly separate DHT's specific contribution from testosterone's, or
        from treatment timing.
  [ON-SCREEN: "OBSERVED, NOT DESIGNED"]

### [0:38–0:52] PAYOFF
SPOKEN: Now compare that to the estrogen cases. Aromatase deficiency and
        estrogen receptor mutation both produced dramatic, unmistakable
        skeletal phenotypes — unfused plates, growing into adulthood. Nothing
        comparably dramatic is reported for lifelong DHT absence.
  [VISUAL: dramatic phenotype vs no clear phenotype]

### [0:52–0:60] CAVEAT + CTA
SPOKEN: That's an argument from absence, and it's weak evidence. But the honest
        read is that DHT's cartilage effects are mechanistically real and
        clinically unquantified. Don't forget, it's never over.
  [ON-SCREEN: "REAL. UNQUANTIFIED."]
