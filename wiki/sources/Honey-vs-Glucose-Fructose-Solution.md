# Source: Honey vs. Honey-Comparable Glucose-Fructose Solution (Münstedt et al. 2008)

**Type**: Randomized crossover trial in healthy subjects (Journal of Medicinal Food, 2008)

## Summary

Directly isolates honey's effect from its sugar content: 12 healthy men were given (1) a standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) solution, (2) basswood/linden honey standardized to 75g glucose, and (3) a "honey-comparable" glucose-fructose solution containing the *exact same* amounts of glucose and fructose as the honey — no other honey constituents. If honey's effect were purely its sugar ratio, honey and the matched sugar solution should behave identically.

## Key Findings

- **Glucose response**: no significant difference between honey and OGTT at any timepoint; honey's AUC was significantly lower than the matched glucose-fructose solution (p<.001).
- **Insulin at 60 min**: honey = 263.9 pmol/L vs. matched sugar solution = 443.1 pmol/L (honey significantly lower).
- **C-peptide at 60 min**: honey = 1.48 mmol/L vs. matched sugar solution = 1.95 mmol/L (honey significantly lower, p<.05).
- **Interpretation**: because both test drinks had *identical* glucose and fructose content, the lower insulin/C-peptide response to real honey cannot be explained by sugar ratio alone. The authors hypothesize honey contains insulin-like or alpha-glucosidase-inhibiting substances, or that minor sugars (trehalose, isomaltose) interfere with glucose/fructose absorption.

## Confidence Note

> [!key-insight] This is one of the cleanest human experimental designs in the [[Health and Nutrition]] domain sources so far — by matching the sugar profile exactly, it isolates "something else in honey" as the active variable, rather than just observing honey has a lower GI than table sugar.

> [!gap] Small sample (n=10 analyzed), healthy subjects only (no diabetic patients), single-dose acute exposure.
