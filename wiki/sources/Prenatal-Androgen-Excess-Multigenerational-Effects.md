# Prenatal Androgen Excess and Multigenerational Effects (Abruzzese et al. 2023)

This paper studies androgen excess from a reproductive-endocrinology/developmental-programming angle distinct from the rest of this domain's growth-plate/bone focus: it's a rat model of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) created by injecting pregnant dams (F0) with testosterone late in gestation, then tracking effects through two subsequent generations (F1 and F2), with maternal and paternal transmission lines evaluated independently.

Prenatally hyperandrogenized (PH) F1 females showed the expected PCOS-like phenotype: altered glucose tolerance, elevated serum triglycerides/cholesterol, and increased anogenital distance (a marker of prenatal androgen exposure), plus a longer time-to-mating despite unchanged fertility/fecundity indices. F1 PH males showed reproductive effects but not metabolic ones, consistent with cited prior literature that prenatal androgen programming effects are sex-dependent and more pronounced in females.

The novel contribution is the F2 (second-generation) analysis, splitting maternal-line (F1-PHf × control male) from paternal-line (control female × F1-PHm) transmission. Both routes produced real, but different, effects: maternal-line F2 offspring were smaller in weight and length, with female-specific glucose and anogenital-distance alterations; paternal-line F2 offspring showed a longer embryonic crown-rump length and altered postnatal body weight patterns. Because the paternal line involved no direct gestational exposure of the F2 to androgen (only the F1 father was originally exposed in utero), the authors interpret this as evidence for genuine paternally-transmitted (likely epigenetic, e.g., sperm small non-coding RNA) programming, not just an artifact of the mother's uterine environment.

> [!gap] Rat model; translation to human PCOS inheritance patterns is not established here. The paper itself notes further studies are needed to identify the specific epigenetic mechanism (sperm ncRNA, DNA methylation, etc.) underlying the paternal effect.
