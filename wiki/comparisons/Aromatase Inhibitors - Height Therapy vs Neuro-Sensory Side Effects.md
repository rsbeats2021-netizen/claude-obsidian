# Aromatase Inhibitors: Height Therapy vs. Neuro-Sensory Side Effects

These two sources both discuss aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole) but come from entirely different corners of medicine, and reading them together highlights something neither paper says explicitly: the same drugs' "side effects" in one population are functionally identical to the intended mechanism in the other.

---

## [[Aromatase-Inhibitors-Near-Final-Height-Males|Bouliari et al. 2026]] — the intended use

72 boys with advanced bone age and compromised predicted height were given letrozole or anastrozole specifically to **block estrogen production in the growth plate**, delaying epiphyseal fusion and allowing more time for longitudinal growth. This worked — letrozole patients gained a median 4.2 cm over anastrozole's 0.8 cm. The paper's safety discussion focuses on liver enzymes, acne, and (briefly, citing other studies) bone density loss — it does not discuss cognitive or sensory effects at all.

## [[Aromatase-in-the-Human-Brain|Azcoitia et al. 2021]] — the same drugs, a different population, the "side effect"

This review discusses aromatase inhibitors as they're used in **postmenopausal breast cancer patients**, where the goal is blocking estrogen production to starve hormone-receptor-positive tumors — bone/growth is irrelevant to that population (postmenopausal). But because the human brain independently expresses aromatase and generates its own local estrogen, these same drugs also block *that* pathway as an unintended consequence, producing:
- Arthralgia and myalgia (documented sensory-system effects)
- Altered retinal function
- Subtle negative effects on verbal/visual memory in some patients

The review specifically notes: *"treatment with the aromatase inhibitor letrozole does not impair the development of cognitive performance in boys"* — directly referencing the pediatric height-therapy population from the Bouliari-type studies, and drawing a contrast with the breast-cancer population where cognitive effects are more reported.

---

## Why the Contrast Is Informative

| | Bouliari et al. (pediatric height) | Azcoitia et al. (breast cancer / brain) |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Adolescent boys | Postmenopausal women |
| Target tissue (intended) | Growth plate cartilage | Breast tumor |
| Duration of use | ~25 months median | Often years (adjuvant therapy) |
| Age-related brain vulnerability | Developing brain, but literature (per Azcoitia) suggests no cognitive impairment | Aging brain, more reported cognitive/sensory effects |
| "Side effect" tissue | Bone density (flagged as the real concern) | Brain (arthralgia, memory, sensory) |

> [!key-insight] The apparent reassurance that AI therapy "doesn't impair cognitive development in boys" (cited in Azcoitia et al.) may reflect a genuinely different physiological context — a still-developing brain compensating differently than an aging one — rather than aromatase inhibition being inherently brain-safe. Neither source directly studies this age-dependent difference; it's an inference from comparing the two literatures side by side.

> [!gap] Bouliari et al.'s cohort had no cognitive/neurological outcome measures at all — so there's no direct evidence either way for this specific population beyond the one citation Azcoitia et al. references. This comparison surfaces a genuine gap: pediatric height-therapy studies of AIs generally don't screen for the sensory/cognitive effects that the breast-cancer AI literature considers standard safety monitoring.
