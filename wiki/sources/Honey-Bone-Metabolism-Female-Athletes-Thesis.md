# Source: Effects of Honey Consumption on Bone Metabolism in Female Athletes (Ahmad 2017, PhD Thesis)

**Type**: PhD thesis, 3 linked human studies (University of Malaya, Sports Centre, 2017) — 166 pages; read strategically (abstract + per-chapter conclusions), not cover-to-cover, given its length relative to the rest of this batch ingest

## Summary

The most substantial **human** study in the [[Health and Nutrition]] domain's bone cluster — a 3-part investigation specifically in female athletes (a population at risk for low BMD due to high training energy expenditure). Study 1 identifies risk factors for low BMD; Study 2 establishes a safe/effective honey dose; Study 3 is an 8-week intervention trial combining honey with jumping exercise.

## Key Findings

**Study 1 (n=85 female athletes, cross-sectional)**:
- 53% had low BMD; mean energy intake only 1291 kcal/day.
- BMD positively associated with Bone Physical Activity Questionnaire (BPAQ) score and body weight; negatively associated with energy expenditure.
- No association found between estrogen, energy intake, or energy availability and BMD — energy expenditure and mechanical loading were the dominant predictors.

**Study 2 (n=20, acute dose-response)**:
- Both low (0.75 g/kg BW) and high (1.5 g/kg BW) Tualang honey doses increased total phenolic content (TPC) and ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) — with no difference between doses.
- Low dose selected for the follow-up intervention trial (equal efficacy, lower sugar/toxicity/allergy risk).

**Study 3 (n=45 female athletes with low BMD, 8-week RCT: honey / honey+jumping / jumping-only)**:
- Honey and honey+jumping groups showed improving *trends* in BMD, bone formation/resorption biomarkers (BAP, ICTP), and estrogen — but differences between the three groups were **not statistically significant**.
- Oxidative stress markers (MDA, ROS) were significantly reduced in honey and honey+jumping groups, and significantly *increased* in the jumping-only group — this was the one statistically robust finding.
- Authors' interpretation: 8 weeks was likely too short to show significant BMD/biomarker changes; the antioxidant/oxidative-stress effect was the clearest signal.

## Confidence Note

> [!gap] This thesis explicitly identifies its own limitation: the 8-week intervention (Study 3) was underpowered/too short to reach significance on its primary bone-density outcomes, with only oxidative stress reaching significance. Read this alongside [[Honey-Bone-Health-Review-2019|Kamaruzzaman et al. 2019]]'s broader observation that human bone-density evidence for honey remains thin — this thesis is a good example of *why*: promising trends, not yet significant effects, likely due to short study duration.

> [!key-insight] Study 1's finding — that energy expenditure and mechanical loading (not estrogen or diet) predicted low BMD in these athletes — is itself a notable standalone result, independent of the honey intervention.
