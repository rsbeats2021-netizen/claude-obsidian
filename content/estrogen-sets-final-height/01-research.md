# Research brief — Estrogen, not testosterone, sets final height

## Core claim / hook angle
A man with entirely normal testosterone never stopped growing, because his
estrogen receptor was broken. Estrogen — not androgen — is what closes the
growth plate.

## Misconception it corrects
Testosterone gets the credit for male puberty, male bone and male height, so
people assume it also ends growth. It's the precursor, not the agent.

## Key points (tier-tagged)
- **Tier 1** — Smith et al. 1994 (NEJM): 28-year-old man, homozygous
  ERα-disrupting mutation. Testosterone normal-to-slightly-elevated, normal
  masculinisation, epiphyses unfused, still growing linearly as an adult.
- **Tier 1** — Built-in control in the same case: six months of estrogen
  therapy, ~10-fold rise in free estradiol, no clinical response. Ligand
  present, receptor broken.
- **Tier 1** — Aromatase inhibitors (letrozole/anastrozole) in boys delay bone
  maturation and increase adult height (Bouliari 2026). The forward test.
- **Tier 1** — Androgens aromatise to estrogen locally *within growth plate
  cartilage* (Shim 2015); cartilage also converts testosterone to DHT locally
  (Takahashi 1984). Cartilage is an active site of hormone conversion.
- **Tier 2 — genuine counter-evidence** — Gunther 2001: fulvestrant blocks the
  estrogen receptor entirely, yet testosterone *still* accelerates skeletal
  maturation in mice. Cannot be explained by aromatisation.
- **Tier 2** — Chou 2025: GPER-1, a third estrogen receptor, promotes
  proliferation and delays hypertrophy — opposite of ERα. Peaks early in
  puberty then declines, suggesting a receptor-subtype handoff over time.

## Why this angle works
The Smith case is the cleanest natural experiment in the whole domain: one
variable changed, dramatic outcome, human, published in NEJM. The built-in
estrogen-therapy control makes it near-airtight. And the deck earns its
credibility by *not* resolving the contested part.

## Deliberately not included
No dosing, no protocol, no aromatase-inhibitor regimen. The AI evidence is
cited as a mechanism test, not as a recommendation — it's prescription therapy
with real bone-density consequences in adolescents.
