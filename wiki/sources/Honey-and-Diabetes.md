# Source: Honey and Diabetes

**Type**: Peer-reviewed review article (Oxidative Medicine and Cellular Longevity, Hindawi, 2018)
**Authors**: Otilia Bobiş, Daniel S. Dezmirean, Adela Ramona Moise (Univ. of Agricultural Sciences and Veterinary Medicine, Cluj-Napoca, Romania)
**DOI**: 10.1155/2018/4757893

## Summary

Review of preclinical (animal) and clinical (human) evidence on honey as a potential antidiabetic dietary agent, and as a topical treatment for diabetic wounds. Concludes honey is a promising but not yet clinically proven adjunct — animal studies are largely favorable, small human trials show a lower glycemic response than sucrose/dextrose, but large-scale RCTs are missing.

## Key Findings

- **Composition**: Honey = 200+ components; ~80% simple sugars (35–40% fructose, 30–35% glucose); GI 58 vs. refined sugar's 60; 300 kcal/100g vs. sugar's 387 kcal/100g.
- **Proposed mechanism**: Fructose stimulates hepatic glucokinase (glucose uptake/storage as glycogen); selective mineral ions (Se, Zn, Cu, V), phenolic acids, and flavonoids may also contribute. Exact mechanism remains unclear.
- **Animal studies**: Mixed — several rat/rabbit studies show reduced blood glucose, HbA1c, and improved lipid profiles with honey vs. sucrose; a few show no significant glucose effect. Honey augmented the antihyperglycemic effect of glibenclamide and metformin in combination.
- **Human studies**: Small trials (20–58 subjects) generally show honey causes a lower rise in plasma glucose than dextrose/sucrose, improves lipid profile (↓LDL, ↑HDL, ↓triglycerides), and lowers CRP/homocysteine. C-peptide response to honey vs. sucrose/glucose is inconsistent across studies.
- **Wound healing**: Honey's acidic pH, hydrogen peroxide, and nitric oxide content give it antimicrobial activity; antioxidants reduce inflammation and reactive oxygen species at wound sites, aiding healing in diabetic ulcers (which heal poorly due to peripheral arterial disease/neuropathy).
- **Limitations acknowledged by authors**: animal models (streptozotocin/alloxan-induced) may not fully reflect human type 2 diabetes; optimal human dosing undetermined; existing human trials are short-duration and small; large multicenter RCTs needed.

## Confidence Note

> [!gap] This is a single review article (not a meta-analysis or RCT) summarizing mostly small/short-duration primary studies. Treat conclusions as suggestive, not definitive — flagged `confidence: medium` in frontmatter.
