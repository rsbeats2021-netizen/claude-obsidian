# Source: Dihydrotestosterone — Biochemistry, Physiology, and Clinical Implications (Swerdloff et al. 2017)

**Type**: Comprehensive review (Endocrine Reviews, 2017) — the most authoritative single source on DHT in this domain cluster

## Summary

DHT (5α-dihydrotestosterone) is the non-aromatizable, more-potent androgen metabolite of testosterone — it cannot be converted to estrogen, making it the "pure androgen" reference point against which aromatization-dependent effects (discussed throughout the rest of this domain) can be contrasted. This review corrects the common misconception that circulating DHT levels matter clinically, and surveys DHT's real tissue-level effects across prostate, skin, body composition, cardiovascular, and bone systems.

## Key Findings

- **Core mechanistic point**: DHT is principally synthesized locally, inside target tissues (prostate, skin, liver), by 5α-reductase acting on testosterone. Intracellular concentrations are what drive androgenic effects and are actively self-regulated (up or down) independent of blood levels — "DHT should be regarded as a paracrine hormone formed and acting primarily within target tissues," not a systemic signal.
- **Bone/body composition (directly relevant to this domain)**: in long-term studies of hypogonadal men, testosterone therapy increased lean mass and BMD regardless of whether finasteride (a 5α-reductase inhibitor) was co-administered — meaning **DHT itself is not required** for testosterone's positive bone/body-composition effects when testosterone is in the normal physiologic range. However, the review explicitly notes "estradiol appears necessary to maximize bone health in men" — i.e., it's the aromatization of testosterone to estrogen, not DHT, that matters most for male bone density. Exogenous supraphysiological DHT, by contrast, can suppress endogenous testosterone/estradiol and has been linked to reduced spinal BMD.
- **Prostate/skin**: DHT is essential for normal prostate development/function and implicated in male-pattern baldness and acne — but even here, it's local (follicular/tissue) DHT concentration that matters, not blood levels.
- **Cardiovascular**: DHT (via AR, not aromatization) promotes nitric oxide synthesis and anti-inflammatory effects in endothelial cells in vitro; clinical/epidemiological evidence for a DHT-specific cardiovascular risk is lacking.
- **Body composition**: supraphysiological DHT modestly increases lean mass and decreases fat mass in some trials, though results are inconsistent across studies (dose and duration-dependent).
- **Safety conclusion**: across all systems reviewed, elevated serum DHT from testosterone replacement therapy has not been shown to carry risks beyond those already known for testosterone itself.

## Confidence Note

> [!key-insight] This is the domain's key evidence for the "estrogen (via aromatization), not androgen directly, drives male bone health" pattern also seen in [[Pubertal-Growth-and-Epiphyseal-Fusion|Shim 2015]] and [[Aromatase-Inhibitors-Near-Final-Height-Males|Bouliari et al. 2026]] — but it directly complicates the picture from [[Androgen-Receptor-Upregulation-Osteoblasts|Wiren et al. 1999]], where DHT (not estradiol) was the specific steroid that up-regulated androgen receptor and enhanced AR-mediated transcription in cultured human osteoblasts. See [[Estrogen vs Androgen in Male Bone Health]] for how these two findings reconcile (in vitro direct-AR mechanism vs. clinical whole-body outcome).

> [!gap] Flagged `confidence: high` — this is a rigorous, extensively-cited Endocrine Society review — but the authors themselves note a lack of long-term, well-powered studies isolating DHT's independent effects from testosterone's.
