Bone Age Explained. Sources in bio.

[cover card]

In mice, epiphyseal fusion, defined as disappearance of the growth plate, occurred at approximately 3 weeks in phalanges and 4 weeks in metacarpals, but had not yet occurred at 12 weeks in tibias or femurs. The same order was observed in rats: 12 weeks in phalanges, 16 weeks in metacarpals, with tibias and femurs still open at 16 weeks.

[dolan et al, plos biology]

At one week of age, the metacarpal bone growth rate was 49% of the tibial rate, and the chondrocyte proliferation rate per column was 46% of the tibial value. All structural and functional markers of growth plate senescence, the progressive decline in proliferative capacity, were more advanced in the metacarpals and proximal phalanges compared with the distal femurs and proximal tibias.

[(and why they matter)]

A cross-sectional MRI study of 958 subjects aged 14 to 21, imaging the wrist, knee and ankle in each participant. Pubertal development (Tanner stage) correlated with growth plate fusion at every site: 0.514 to 0.598 in males, 0.287 to 0.513 in females.

[kvist et al 2021, acta paediatrica]

BMI correlated with growth plate maturity, 0.186 to 0.384, higher in males. Physical activity showed no significant correlation in males at any site, and in females only a very low correlation at the distal radius (0.095), with no other site reaching significance.

[what the correlations showed]

Interpret bone age as an estimate derived from the fastest-maturing bones in the body, not as a direct readout of long bone status. Track pubertal stage, which correlated with fusion at every site imaged.

[takeaway card]

The hand matures first, in every dataset examined.
