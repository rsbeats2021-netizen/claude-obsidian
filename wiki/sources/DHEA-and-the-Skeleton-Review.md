# DHEA and the Skeleton (Through the Ages) (Gordon et al. 1999)

A comprehensive review of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and DHEAS — the most abundant circulating steroids in humans — and their role across skeletal development, maintenance, and age-related bone loss. DHEA(S) levels rise sharply through puberty, peak in the third decade, then decline steadily to 10-20% of peak by age 70, a phenomenon the review terms "adrenopause" (per Fuller Albright's original 1947 coinage).

DHEA(S) functions primarily as a circulating precursor reservoir: roughly 50% of adult male androgens and up to nearly 100% of postmenopausal female estrogens are synthesized locally in peripheral tissues (bone, fat, muscle, skin, brain) from DHEA(S), via tissue-specific expression of aromatase, 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, and 5α-reductase. Critically, the review documents *both* a direct, AR-mediated action of DHEA on human osteoblasts (stimulating proliferation and alkaline phosphatase activity, both blockable — the former by the AR antagonist hydroxyflutamide, the latter by neutralizing TGF-β antibodies) and an indirect route via local aromatization to estrogen and via stimulating the skeletal IGF-I regulatory system.

A specific cytokine mechanism is proposed to link DHEA decline to age-related bone loss: DHEA, DHT, and testosterone all suppress IL-6 (a major pro-resorptive, osteoclast-activating cytokine) in bone marrow stromal cell cultures, but this suppression requires AR expression in the target cells — directly paralleling the mechanism by which estrogen suppresses IL-6 via ER, described in the classic Jilka et al. 1992 ovariectomized-mouse work cited in this review.

Clinical trial evidence reviewed: oral DHEA (50 mg/day) in postmenopausal women, elderly men, and adolescents with anorexia nervosa consistently raised serum IGF-I and reduced urinary bone-resorption markers, with topical DHEA producing a measurable 2.3% increase in hip/spine bone density over 1 year in one preliminary study — though the review explicitly notes long-term administration effects on bone mass had not yet been studied at time of writing.

> [!key-insight] This is the clearest single source in the domain establishing that a single steroid precursor (DHEA) can have simultaneous direct-AR, aromatization-to-estrogen, and IGF-I-mediated anabolic effects on bone — a three-pathway convergence model that helps reconcile the domain's broader "is it androgen or estrogen doing the work" tension (see [[Estrogen vs Androgen in Male Bone Health]]): for DHEA specifically, the honest answer may be "both, through different mechanisms simultaneously."

> [!gap] A 1999 review; much of the in vitro mechanistic evidence (IL-6 suppression, AR-mediated proliferation) comes from small, sometimes inconsistent studies the authors themselves flag as needing replication — and long-term DHEA supplementation trials the review calls for were largely not pursued at scale in the following decades.
