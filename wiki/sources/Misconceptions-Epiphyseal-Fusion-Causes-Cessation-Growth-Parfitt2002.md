# Epiphyseal Fusion Doesn't Cause Growth Cessation — It's the Other Way Around (Parfitt 2002)

A landmark perspective piece directly challenging a causal assumption implicit throughout much of this domain's growth-plate-closure literature: Parfitt argues that the widely-held belief — "growth stops because the epiphysis fuses" — reverses the actual causal order. Longitudinal growth ceases first, because the growth plate's proliferative capacity is exhausted (consistent with the finite-capacity/senescence model this domain already tracks via [[Epiphyseal Skeletal Stem Cells and Hedgehog Signaling]]); epiphyseal fusion then follows as a consequence of that cessation, essentially "cleaning up" a growth plate that has already stopped functioning, rather than being the active mechanism that shuts growth down.

> [!contradiction] This directly complicates the framing used by many other sources in this domain (and, implicitly, by earlier synthesis in [[Epiphyseal Fusion Mechanisms]]) that treat "fusion" and "cessation of growth" as effectively the same event, or treat fusion as the causal trigger for growth stopping. If Parfitt is right, then interventions or hormonal manipulations that are described as "delaying fusion to allow more growth" (e.g., aromatase inhibitors) should more accurately be understood as delaying the exhaustion of proliferative capacity — with fusion itself being a downstream, largely inconsequential-to-height marker of that exhaustion having already occurred. This reframes a significant fraction of this domain's height-intervention literature.

## Relevance to This Domain

A foundational conceptual correction for [[Epiphyseal Fusion Mechanisms]] — challenges the implicit assumption that fusion causes growth cessation, arguing instead that proliferative exhaustion causes cessation and fusion merely follows, with significant implications for how this domain's height-intervention literature (aromatase inhibitors, etc.) should be interpreted.
