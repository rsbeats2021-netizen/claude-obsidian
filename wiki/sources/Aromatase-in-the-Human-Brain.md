# Source: Aromatase in the Human Brain (Azcoitia et al. 2021)

**Type**: Review article (Androgens: Clinical Research and Therapeutics, 2021)

## Summary

While [[Pubertal-Growth-and-Epiphyseal-Fusion|Shim 2015]] and [[Aromatase-Inhibitors-Near-Final-Height-Males|Bouliari et al. 2026]] treat aromatase as the enzyme that closes growth plates, this review is about the *same enzyme* doing something completely different: locally converting androgens to estrogen inside the brain itself, independent of circulating hormone levels, with effects on cognition, mood, sensory processing, and neurodegeneration.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution**: aromatase (CYP19A1) is expressed in neurons (perikaryon, dendrites, axons, synaptic terminals), astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, ependymal cells, and choroid plexus cells across nearly all examined brain regions — highest in thalamus, amygdala, and hypothalamus by PET imaging. Similar expression pattern in men and women (unlike the strong sex differences seen in rodent brain aromatase).
- **Thalamus (sensory integration)**: aromatase PET signal correlates with cooperativeness scores; consistent with the fact that cancer patients on aromatase inhibitors often develop arthralgia/myalgia — a sensory-system effect.
- **Amygdala/hypothalamus (social behavior, mood)**: aromatase levels associate with aggression, novelty-seeking, and cooperativeness in sex-specific patterns; aromatase is significantly *decreased* in the hypothalamus of major depressive disorder patients.
- **Cortex/hippocampus (cognition/language)**: aromatase expression here plausibly supports learning/memory via local estradiol; some CYP19A1 gene variants correlate with memory-test performance and dyslexia traits; AIs used in cancer treatment have documented (subtle) negative cognitive effects, though letrozole specifically does not appear to impair cognitive development in boys.
- **Neuroprotection & disease**: aromatase expression increases in astrocytes after stroke and traumatic brain injury (a proposed protective response via local estradiol), and is elevated in multiple sclerosis lesions and in the hippocampus/prefrontal cortex of Alzheimer's patients — though causality/benefit vs. harm of this upregulation is unresolved.
- **Aromatase and epilepsy — a genuine paradox noted by the authors**: estradiol increases seizure *frequency* but reduces seizure *severity/duration* and protects against neuronal damage — so aromatase inhibitors reduce seizure frequency but *increase* hippocampal damage from seizures in animal models. Not a simple "more estrogen bad" or "more estrogen good" story.

## Confidence Note

> [!gap] Much of the mechanistic detail (neuroprotection, cognition specifics) comes from animal studies extrapolated to humans; the authors repeatedly state human-specific confirmation is lacking for many claims (e.g., no human data at all on aromatase in the spinal cord).

> [!key-insight] This paper is the clearest illustration in this domain of why "aromatase inhibitor" isn't a bone-specific or cancer-specific drug class — see [[Aromatase Across Tissues]] for how the same enzyme's inhibition plays out completely differently depending on which tissue you're targeting (bone growth plates, brain, breast tumor, cardiovascular endothelium).
