# ERα in Growth Plate Cartilage: Direct vs. Indirect Action (Börjesson et al. 2010)

An earlier paper from the same Gothenburg/Karolinska research group behind [[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]], asking a more foundational question first: does estrogen act on the growth plate *directly* via ERα expressed in cartilage cells, or *indirectly* via the systemic GH/IGF-1 axis? Using a cartilage-specific ERα inactivation mouse model (distinct from the total-body knockout used in the 2012 paper), the authors found skeletal growth was *normal* during sexual maturation — in contrast to total-body ERα inactivation, which produces the well-known prolonged-growth phenotype via GH/IGF-1 axis alterations.

> [!contradiction] This creates a genuine interpretive tension with the same group's 2012 paper already in this domain. If cartilage-specific ERα loss doesn't disrupt growth during maturation, but total-body ERα loss does (via GH/IGF-1 changes), then [[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]]'s AF-1-specific closure-acceleration phenotype may need to be read cautiously — some of estrogen's growth-plate effect may run through systemic hormone axes rather than being a cartilage-autonomous ERα mechanism, a distinction the 2012 paper doesn't fully address since it also used whole-body genetic models.

> [!gap] Mouse model; exactly how the cartilage-specific vs. total-body ERα models differ mechanistically (e.g., whether the cartilage-specific knockout construct fully eliminates ERα function) isn't detailed in the extracted abstract.

## Relevance to This Domain

Adds an important direct-vs-indirect distinction to [[Estrogen Receptor (ER)]] and [[Three Estrogen Receptor Mechanisms in the Growth Plate]] — complicates rather than simply extends [[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]] by suggesting some of ERα's apparent growth-plate effect may be mediated systemically via GH/IGF-1 rather than cartilage-autonomously.
