# GPER-1 Facilitates Chondrocyte Proliferation via PTHrP/Ihh Regulation (Chou et al. 2025)

This paper adds a third distinct estrogen receptor to this domain's coverage — GPER-1 (G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor-1, a membrane receptor mediating rapid non-genomic signaling), alongside the classical nuclear ERα and ERβ already covered via [[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]]. Unlike ERα (whose activation ultimately *promotes* growth-plate closure per Börjesson et al.), GPER-1 activation here *promotes* chondrocyte proliferation and *delays* hypertrophic differentiation — an apparently opposite direction of effect from the same broad hormone class, mediated through a completely different receptor and mechanism.

Using three independent methods — a selective GPER-1 agonist (G1), a selective antagonist (G15), and chondrocyte-specific genetic knockout — the authors converge on a consistent mechanism: GPER-1 signaling increases the ratio of PTHrP to Indian hedgehog (Ihh) expression in growth-plate chondrocytes. Since PTHrP is known to maintain chondrocyte proliferation and delay the transition to hypertrophy (with Ihh normally counteracting this via negative feedback), an increased PTHrP/Ihh ratio directly explains the observed phenotype: more proliferation, less hypertrophy, thicker growth plates, modestly longer bones — a sex-independent effect confirmed in both male and female mice. The effect was replicated in isolated micromass 3D chondrocyte cultures, ruling out systemic confounds.

GPER-1 expression peaks specifically in early-to-mid puberty (4-8 weeks in mice) and declines toward sexual maturity, as estrogen levels rise — the authors propose that low pubertal estrogen levels act preferentially through GPER-1 to promote growth, while the later estrogen surge shifts the balance toward classical ER-mediated effects (including the closure-promoting ERα mechanism).

> [!contradiction] This paper's GPER-1-promotes-proliferation/delays-hypertrophy finding sits in an interesting tension with [[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]]'s finding that ERα signaling ultimately drives growth-plate *closure*. Neither paper directly engages the other, but together they suggest estrogen's net effect on the growth plate may depend heavily on which receptor subtype is dominant at a given pubertal stage — GPER-1 early (pro-growth), ERα later (pro-closure) — a genuinely different resolution of the "estrogen dose/timing" puzzle than the growth-plate-senescence-acceleration model proposed in [[Growth-Plate-Senescence-Epiphyseal-Fusion-Review|Nilsson & Baron 2004]].

> [!gap] Mouse model; the authors themselves note they did not investigate interactions between GPER-1 and the classical ERs (ERα/ERβ) in the same tissue, so how these receptor systems integrate their sometimes-opposing signals within the same growth plate remains unresolved.
