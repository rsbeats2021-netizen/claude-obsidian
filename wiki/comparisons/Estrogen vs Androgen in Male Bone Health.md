# Estrogen vs. Androgen in Male Bone Health

A recurring question across four sources in this domain: when androgens affect male bone, is it really the androgen doing the work, or is it estrogen — produced from androgen via aromatase — doing the work under an androgen's name? The four papers here don't fully agree on emphasis, and one directly complicates the tidy "it's all estrogen" story.

---

## The "it's really estrogen" camp

**[[Pubertal-Growth-and-Epiphyseal-Fusion|Shim 2015]]** states this most explicitly: "In epiphyseal fusion, the activity of androgen is due to the aromatization of androgens to estrogens in various peripheral tissues, including growth plate cartilage." Androgen doesn't get credit for a direct effect here — it's a precursor.

**[[Aromatase-Inhibitors-Near-Final-Height-Males|Bouliari et al. 2026]]** operationalizes this claim clinically: blocking aromatase (thus blocking androgen→estrogen conversion) with letrozole/anastrozole reliably delays bone maturation and increases adult height in boys — a direct clinical test of the "estrogen closes growth plates" hypothesis, and it works.

**[[Dihydrotestosterone-Clinical-Implications|Swerdloff et al. 2017]]** reaches the same conclusion from an adult-male body-composition/BMD angle: testosterone therapy with a 5α-reductase inhibitor (finasteride, which blocks DHT production but leaves aromatization intact) still produced the expected gains in lean mass and bone mineral density. In other words, DHT wasn't necessary — but presumably the un-blocked aromatization of testosterone to estradiol was. The review states outright: "estradiol appears necessary to maximize bone health in men."

**[[Estrogen-Resistance-ERalpha-Mutation-Man-Smith1994-NEJM|Smith et al. 1994, NEJM]]** supplies the foundational human case this entire camp ultimately rests on: a 28-year-old man with a homozygous ERα-disrupting mutation had completely normal (even slightly elevated) testosterone and normal masculinization, yet his epiphyses never fused and he kept growing linearly into adulthood — with six months of estrogen therapy producing no clinical response at all despite a 10-fold rise in free estradiol. This is the cleanest possible separation of the two hormones: androgen entirely normal, only the estrogen receptor broken, and the growth plate simply doesn't close.

**[[Testosterone-Estradiol-DHEA-Skeletal-Effects-Review|A comprehensive review of gonadal and adrenal androgens]]** (testosterone, DHEA, DHEA-sulfate, androstenedione) across the lifespan reaches the same estradiol-dominant conclusion after surveying both androgen sources, adding breadth (adrenal, not just gonadal, androgens) to this camp's evidence base.

## The complicating cell-culture finding

**[[Androgen-Receptor-Upregulation-Osteoblasts|Wiren et al. 1999]]** doesn't fit neatly into the above. Working directly with human osteoblastic cell lines, they found that **DHT** — the one androgen that *cannot* be aromatized to estrogen — specifically up-regulated androgen receptor levels and dramatically amplified the cell's transcriptional response to androgen. Testosterone matched DHT's effect; estradiol, notably, did **not** increase AR levels at all in this experiment. This is a real, receptor-specific, non-aromatization-dependent androgen effect on bone cells, demonstrated directly rather than inferred.

**[[DHT-Prevents-Glucocorticoid-Effects-Fetal-Bone|Picherit et al. 2000]]** adds another direct-androgen result: DHT, added to cultured fetal rat metatarsal bones, prevented glucocorticoid-induced suppression of growth and mineralization, and had a modest stimulatory effect on its own.

**[[Testosterone-Metabolism-Rabbit-Cartilage|Takahashi et al. 1984]]** shows the biochemical precondition for all of this exists: both articular and growth-plate cartilage actively metabolize testosterone into DHT locally, with DHT as the dominant accumulated metabolite — cartilage is not a passive target of circulating hormone but an active site of local androgen conversion.

**[[Sex-Steroid-Regulation-Cartilage-Bone-Proliferation|Somjen et al. 1991]]** complicates the picture further with a tissue-specific split: cultured bone cells respond to sex steroids in a sex-specific way (estradiol only stimulates female-derived bone cells, androgen only male-derived), but cultured cartilage cells respond to **both** estradiol and DHT regardless of donor sex. Bone and cartilage, in other words, may not follow the same rules.

## The Direct Counter-Evidence

**[[Androgen-Receptor-Modulation-No-Effect-Growth-Plate|Chagin et al. 2009]]** tested testosterone and the non-aromatizable androgen oxandrolone in the same organ-culture system Picherit used, and found **no effect** on baseline longitudinal growth rate. Notably, this paper explicitly discusses Picherit et al. 2000 (cited directly as prior work) and argues the two findings are not strictly contradictory: preventing glucocorticoid-induced suppression (Picherit's result) is a different claim from actively promoting baseline growth (what Chagin tested and did not find). Still, this is the rare case in this domain where two primary sources on the same narrow question directly cite and grapple with each other, rather than the tension being purely the wiki's own synthesis.

**[[AR-Overexpression-Chondrogenic-Rabbit-Articular-Chondrocytes|Liu et al. 2021]]** adds a further wrinkle: AR overexpression clearly benefits **articular** chondrocytes (reduced apoptosis, better matrix gene expression, improved cartilage-defect regeneration in vivo), but via reduced apoptosis and improved phenotype maintenance, not increased proliferation — and the authors explicitly attribute the lack of an AR-driven proliferation effect (which contrasts with AR's reported growth-plate role) to a genuine tissue difference between articular and growth-plate chondrocytes, rather than viewing it as inconsistent with the growth-plate literature.

**[[Androgen-Bone-Maturation-Not-Attenuated-Faslodex-Gunther2001|Gunther, Underwood & Calikoglu 2001]]** supplies perhaps the cleanest direct test in this domain: testosterone-accelerated skeletal maturation in mice was **not attenuated by Faslodex** (fulvestrant), a pure ER blocker/degrader eliminating all estrogen-receptor signaling regardless of aromatization. Unlike the aromatase-inhibitor and 5α-reductase-inhibitor studies elsewhere in this comparison (which infer aromatization-dependence indirectly, by blocking hormone conversion), this study blocks the estrogen receptor itself and still sees the androgen effect — direct evidence favoring the "it's really AR" side, at least for this specific bone-maturation endpoint.

## The Estrogen Receptor's Own Internal Complexity

**[[ERalpha-AF1-Growth-Plate-Closure-Female-Mice|Börjesson et al. 2012]]** shows that even on the "estrogen dominates" side of this comparison, the mechanism is not simple: complete ERα knockout mice have prolonged growth and wide-open growth plates (matching the human aromatase-deficient/estrogen-resistant phenotype), but mice missing only ERα's AF-1 activation domain show the *opposite* — accelerated closure via increased chondrocyte apoptosis. This means ERα's growth-plate-closing function specifically does NOT require AF-1, and AF-1 normally opposes/delays closure — a level of mechanistic detail this domain does not yet have for the androgen-receptor side of the comparison.

Adding further nuance, **[[GPER1-Chondrocyte-Proliferation-PTHrP-Ihh|Chou et al. 2025]]** shows that a *third* estrogen receptor, GPER-1, does the apparent opposite of ERα: it promotes chondrocyte proliferation and delays hypertrophy (via raising PTHrP/Ihh) rather than driving closure. GPER-1 expression peaks in early puberty and declines as classical estrogen signaling rises later — suggesting estrogen's net growth-plate effect may depend on a receptor-subtype handoff over pubertal time (GPER-1-dominant pro-growth early, ERα-dominant pro-closure later), a distinct resolution of the estrogen-timing puzzle from the proliferative-exhaustion model in [[Growth-Plate-Senescence-Epiphyseal-Fusion-Review|Nilsson & Baron 2004]].

## Reconciling the Views

The evidence now spans more than just "bench vs. bedside" — it includes disagreement within the bench-level evidence itself:

- Multiple in vitro/organ-culture studies (Wiren, Picherit, Takahashi, Somjen) collectively show that **direct, non-aromatized androgen signaling on bone and cartilage cells is real and mechanistically plausible** — the receptors, the local metabolism machinery, and measurable cellular responses all exist.
- One organ-culture study (Chagin) using a similar model system found **no effect of AR activation on longitudinal growth rate specifically**, and explicitly engages with Picherit's earlier, different result rather than ignoring it.
- Shim, Bouliari, and Swerdloff are all describing **whole-body clinical/physiological outcomes**, where the *net* effect of interfering with aromatization is large and consistent, while direct-androgen contributions appear smaller or non-dominant in the aggregate outcome.
- Somjen's bone-vs-cartilage split suggests part of the disagreement may be tissue-specific: androgen's direct effect may be more consistent in cartilage than in bone proper, and "growth-plate longitudinal growth rate" (what Chagin measured) may not capture the same thing as "bone mineralization/rescue-from-suppression" (what Picherit and Wiren measured).

## Whole-Animal Confirmation: Synergy AND Opposition, Simultaneously

**[[DHT-Estrogen-Combined-Bone-Density-Growth-Ovariectomized-Rats|Coxam et al. 1996]]** adds rigorous whole-animal (ovariectomized rat) data using the exact same DHT-isolates-the-androgen-effect logic as Wiren and Picherit, but at the organism level with full histomorphometry. The result complicates a simple "androgen vs. estrogen" framing further: DHT and estrogen were *synergistic* on bone mineral density and calcium accretion (combined treatment beat either alone) while simultaneously *opposing* on longitudinal growth-plate-driven bone elongation (DHT partially reversed estrogen's growth-suppressing effect). The same two hormones can cooperate on one bone outcome and compete on another, in the same animals, in the same experiment.

> [!key-insight] Put plainly: androgens likely *do* have a direct, AR-mediated effect on bone and cartilage cells in several assay systems, but at the level of whole-organism growth-plate closure and adult bone density, aromatization to estrogen appears to be the dominant lever. Whether direct AR signaling meaningfully drives longitudinal growth specifically remains genuinely contested even among cell-/organ-culture studies (Picherit vs. Chagin), not just between bench and clinical literature.

> [!gap] Shim's review predates and undergirds the mechanistic assumption both Bouliari and Swerdloff build on; Wiren et al. 1999, Picherit et al. 2000, and Takahashi et al. 1984 are all substantially older than the clinical papers, and none of the clinical-outcome sources explicitly engages with this cluster of in vitro findings. The Picherit/Chagin exchange is the one place in this cluster where sources cite each other directly — everywhere else, the tension is the wiki's own synthesis across independently-conducted studies.
