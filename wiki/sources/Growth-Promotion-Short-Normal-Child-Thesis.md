# Growth Promotion in the Short Normal Child (Stirling 1994, MD Thesis)

A large (343,000-character) 1994 MD thesis from the University of Liverpool, read strategically via its abstract, summary, and conclusions given its length — the same approach used earlier in this vault for the Ahmad 2017 honey thesis. Presents three separate randomized/controlled clinical studies plus a psychological assessment, evaluating growth-promoting agents in "short normal" children — those who don't meet classical growth-hormone-deficiency criteria but might still benefit from treatment, a genuinely open clinical question at the time recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) first became available in "unlimited" supply.

**Study 1** (prepubertal familial short stature, n=37, double-blind placebo-controlled): rhGH clearly accelerated short-term growth (7.67 cm/yr vs. 4.76 placebo / 4.83 no-treatment), but at a cost — puberty onset and progression were advanced, especially in girls, meaning the growth window itself may close sooner.

**Study 2** (peri-pubertal boys with familial short stature, n=43, randomized): compares rhGH, the anabolic androgen oxandrolone, their combination, and control. All three active arms grew faster than control, with the combination arm (9.92 cm/yr) outperforming either agent alone (rhGH 7.58, oxandrolone 8.08 cm/yr) — but oxandrolone-containing groups showed earlier puberty onset and *faster skeletal maturation*, a direct clinical demonstration of the risk this domain's [[Epiphyseal Fusion Mechanisms|growth-plate-closure]] literature would predict: accelerating an anabolic-androgen-driven growth spurt can accelerate the very process (epiphyseal maturation) that ends it.

**Study 3** (boys with puberty delay, n=33, randomized): rhGH vs. oral testosterone undecanoate vs. combination — essentially a dead heat (8.59, 8.48, 9.91 cm/yr, no significant difference in growth or pubertal progression). The thesis concludes there is no advantage to rhGH over direct androgen replacement in this population, a notable finding given rhGH's far greater cost and treatment burden.

**Study 4** (psychological): short-stature children were not clinically disturbed as a group but scored higher on hyperactivity measures; active treatment (especially rhGH) modestly improved self-esteem and behavior ratings.

> [!key-insight] Study 2's oxandrolone/skeletal-maturation finding is a rare clinical (not cell-culture or animal-model) demonstration of the tension this domain's other anabolic-steroid sources describe more abstractly: [[Nandrolone-Achondroplasia-Case-Report|Al-Mosawi 2006]]'s single-patient nandrolone case saw dramatic growth benefit without this thesis's controlled comparison group, while this larger, controlled study shows the same drug class trades faster short-term growth for faster bone-age advancement — direct supporting evidence for treating [[Testosterone|anabolic-androgen growth promotion]] as a genuinely dose/timing-dependent risk-benefit tradeoff rather than a simple net positive.

> [!gap] A single-institution 1994 thesis; final adult height outcomes (as opposed to short-term growth velocity) are not reported in the material read, and the author's own conclusion is explicit that short-term acceleration doesn't reliably translate to final height gain. Read via abstract/summary/conclusions rather than the full body text given its length (>340,000 characters).

## Relevance to This Domain

A rigorous three-study clinical comparison directly extending [[Growth Hormone-IGF-1 Axis]] (rhGH vs. anabolic steroid vs. combination vs. control, with actual growth-velocity numbers across three distinct pediatric populations) and [[Testosterone]] (oxandrolone and testosterone undecanoate as two more anabolic-androgen growth-promotion agents, joining nandrolone/Deca-Durabolin in this domain's risk-benefit spectrum). Also supplies the clearest clinical-trial-level evidence in this domain for [[Epiphyseal Fusion Mechanisms|accelerated skeletal maturation]] as a real cost of anabolic-androgen-driven growth promotion, not just a theoretical concern.
