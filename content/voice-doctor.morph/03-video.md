Bone age. Lets go through the studies.

[cover card]

A very important study, Dolan et al in PLOS Biology, looked at epiphyseal fusion, which is the growth plate closing for good, in different bones of the same animal. The tibia and femur had still not fused at 12 weeks.

[different bones, different clocks]

They repeated it in rats and got the exact same order. 12 weeks in the phalanges, 16 weeks in the metacarpals, and the tibias and femurs still open at 16.

[this is not a mouse thing]

Now here is the part most people miss. At one week old, the metacarpal bone growth rate was 49% of the tibial rate, and the proliferation rate per column, meaning how many times the cells in one column of the growth plate divide, was 46%.

[they also grow at different speeds]

Now lets look at the human data. Kvist 2021 did MRI on 958 people aged 14 to 21 and imaged the wrist, knee and ankle in every person.

[kvist 2021]

Pubertal stage correlated with fusion at every site they imaged. Wrist 0.596, upper shin 0.597, ankle 0.598, heel 0.514 in males, lower but present in females.

[puberty, not age]

BMI correlated with growth plate maturity, 0.186 to 0.384. Physical activity had no significant correlation in males at any site, and in females one very low correlation at the wrist, 0.095, nothing else.

[training vs body composition]

So from these studies we know three things. Puberty stage tracks fusion better than anything else measured.
